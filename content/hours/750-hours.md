## Snapshot

- Main input: Dreaming Spanish, YouTube
- Daily input: ~2 hours 16 minutes (50 hours over 22 days)
- Other activities: Weekly 50-minute conversation lessons, occasional reading and unassisted writing

---

## What's Improved

I don't have any dramatic breakthroughs to report this time. I've mostly just been quietly accumulating hours, listening every day, and getting on with everything else in life.

But I do feel increasingly confident about my comprehension. Lately I've been feeling particularly locked in when listening where I feel like it takes less cognitive effort to follow along even fast speech. I'm not sure whether that's because my comprehension has improved, because I'm doing a better job of paying attention, or some combination of the two.

When I'm focused, I generally feel confident that I'll understand most of what I'm watching. There are still plenty of exceptions, of course, but that confidence is becoming more dependable.

One thing I've noticed is that the nature of my comprehension gaps has changed. A few hundred hours ago, I'd frequently lose entire sentences or large chunks of speech. Sometimes I'd miss so much that recovering the meaning was impossible.

Now, more often, I'll understand most of a sentence but miss a particular noun, verb, or expression. In one of [Michelle's street interviews on Dreaming Spanish][michelle-street-interview], for example, someone mentioned *la minería a cielo abierto* (open-pit mining). I didn't know the expression, but I understood much of the surrounding language. A quick lookup helped clarify things (and is how I learn some new vocabulary) without breaking the flow of my viewing.

I've always loved these street interviews. Michelle used to be one of the more challenging guides for me (it's amazing how much repeated exposure to difficult speakers dramatically improves things), and I've deliberately watched quite a few of her videos. They're invaluable for hearing everyday people with different accents, speech patterns, and ways of expressing themselves. It's a way of taking the training wheels off, at least a little.

Something else I've noticed is that comprehension still takes effort, but noticeably less than it did several hundred hours ago. It's not effortless, and certainly not equivalent to listening to English, but the amount of mental work required seems to be gradually diminishing.

I'm noticing something similar with reading.

I've started [*Antes de ser libres*][ammo-adsl] by Julia Alvarez, and I'm a little over two chapters into it. Reading feels smoother than it ever has before. There are unfamiliar words here and there, but they're frequently individual nouns or verbs that I can look up without disrupting the flow of the story.

And it's a good story! I'm becoming increasingly invested in Anita, the young narrator, and her family's situation under the Trujillo dictatorship in the Dominican Republic. The growing atmosphere of fear, surveillance, and whispered conversations is unsettling, particularly when experienced through the eyes of a girl who is only beginning to understand what's happening around her.

I'm reading slowly, but I'm getting into the story rather than constantly struggling to decode the language. That's a welcome change.

## What's Still Hard

Native speakers can still be extraordinarily difficult to understand.

I tried watching [Pedro Sánchez's interview with David Broncano on *La Revuelta*][pedro-sanchez-la-revuelta], and it was a struggle. The rapid speech and unfamiliar vocabulary left me grasping for the general meaning rather than comfortably following the conversation.

I also attended an international festival at my daughter's school, where my father-in-law was helping at a booth for Spain. He was exchanging *chistes verdes* with another Spaniard, and I couldn't understand the jokes in real time. They relied on unfamiliar vocabulary and puns.

Of course, understanding wordplay is a demanding skill. It's not enough to get the gist. You need to catch the precise words, recognize multiple meanings, and process the joke quickly enough for it to make sense.

These experiences have also reminded me that there's a difference between understanding a street interview and following an unpredictable conversation between native speakers. Street interviews still provide some scaffolding: a question establishes the direction of the answer. A dynamic conversation doesn't necessarily offer that luxury.

And then there's speaking.

**Me cuesta muchísimo hablar español.**

I've been taking regular 50-minute conversation lessons (these evolved from about a month of crosstalk lessons), and speaking remains incredibly difficult. I struggle to retrieve words, conjugate verbs, arrange pronouns, and construct sentences quickly enough to communicate what I'm thinking.

Sometimes I become painfully aware of the errors I'm making. I'll be trying to express an idea while another part of my brain is busy telling me that I've used the wrong conjugation or misplaced a pronoun. It can become difficult to keep my thoughts moving forward.

I can work through many of these problems when writing, given enough time. But spontaneous conversation is another beast entirely.

There's a frustrating gap between the Spanish I can understand and the Spanish I can produce. There's also an enormous gap between my current abilities and the imaginary future version of myself who speaks Spanish comfortably and naturally.

Some days I feel confident. Other days, a difficult conversation reminds me just how far I have to go.

It's a continually humbling experience.

## What I'm Doing Now

Listening remains the foundation of my routine, but I've been experimenting with speaking, writing, and reading alongside it.

I've continued taking 50-minute conversation lessons with my tutor from Spain. Before several of these lessons, I've worried about whether I'd even be able to keep the conversation going for the entire session.

And yet, every time, we've managed it. We've discussed artificial intelligence, mathematics, regional differences within Spain, humor, politics, and whatever else comes up.

In one lesson, I tried explaining some of the ideas from [a lecture about artificial intelligence and mathematics][ai-mathematics] that I'd recently watched. Attempting to discuss such subjects in Spanish is a rather effective way to discover how much vocabulary and grammatical fluency I still lack!

My tutor also provides excellent written feedback afterward, identifying recurring patterns in my mistakes and supplying review questions. I've found that particularly valuable.

On his suggestion, I've also begun writing short passages in Spanish, usually around 150–200 words. I write without looking anything up, then review corrections afterward to identify grammatical mistakes and more natural ways of expressing myself.

I really enjoy this. Writing gives me the time to think about how I'm constructing sentences without the pressure of someone waiting for me to respond. I haven't been entirely consistent with it lately, but I'd like to continue doing this indefinitely.

I've also occasionally been watching grammar explanations entirely in Spanish, including [this video about the subjunctive][subjunctive] from a Spanish content creator.

One unexpected development is that speaking practice has changed the way I listen and read.

Now that I'm actively trying to produce Spanish, I find myself paying closer attention to the grammatical structures I encounter during input. I'm noticing how pronouns connect to verbs, how *que* links different parts of sentences, and when native speakers use the subjunctive.

I think part of this is that I now have enough mental bandwidth to notice these details while still following the meaning. But I also have a more immediate reason to care about them: they're precisely the things I'm struggling to produce myself.

Instead of simply understanding a sentence, I sometimes find myself wondering how I might use a similar construction to express my own thoughts.

It's making my input more engaging, and I hadn't necessarily expected that from starting to speak.

## Notable Moments

One of my favorite conversation lessons involved humor. I deliberately challenged myself not to talk about artificial intelligence for once, and instead we discussed comedians, jokes, childhood experiences, and the different kinds of humor I enjoy with my family.

I even shared a few *chistes verdes* I'd learned from my father-in-law, including the ridiculous:

*No es lo mismo un tubérculo que ver tu culo.*

It was a genuinely fun conversation.

And during that lesson, I noticed something encouraging: there were a few brief moments when Spanish seemed to come out naturally.

I want to emphasize *brief*. I'm not suddenly speaking fluently. But there were little stretches when I wasn't fighting quite so hard to assemble every sentence.

Another thing I've noticed is that learning Spanish has made me want to be more friendly and talkative with people.

That's interesting to me because I don't always feel that way in English. Sometimes speaking English feels like a performance, as though I ought to demonstrate my intelligence or verbal acuity. That's an entirely unnecessary pressure, but it's there nonetheless.

Spanish feels different, at least for now.

Even though I have far fewer expressive abilities in Spanish, the simple act of communicating has become interesting and compelling. I find myself wanting to talk to people, even when I know I'm going to struggle.

I hope that feeling lasts.

---

## Takeaway

I've been reflecting lately on my relationship with progress and metrics.

I've noticed this tendency in other interests, particularly running and chess. When an activity has a number, rating, or level attached to it, I can become too invested in improving that metric, sometimes at the expense of enjoying the activity itself.

Language learning is no exception.

I'm excited about reaching 750 hours, and it's remarkable to realize that I could be approaching or exceeding 800 hours by the time I visit family in Miami around New Year's.

But there's nothing magical about 750 or 800 hours. Reaching either number doesn't guarantee fluency or some particular level of ability.

I don't need to speedrun my way through this. I want to accumulate quality input, remain attentive, and enjoy the process rather than merely watching the hours increase.

I'm also trying to approach my upcoming trip to Miami without unnecessary expectations. I want to communicate with my family and strangers in Spanish, not necessarily fluently, but with whatever abilities I've developed by then. It'll be an opportunity to use the language and discover where I am.

If I struggle, so what?

This is an enormous, long-term undertaking. Nobody is expecting me to reach some arbitrary standard. Any pressure to do so is pressure I'm putting on myself.

Looking back over these 50 hours, I haven't experienced some spectacular transformation. Instead, I'm noticing something quieter: comprehension is becoming more dependable, reading is becoming smoother, and Spanish is gradually demanding less effort to understand.

Speaking remains hard. Very hard. But I'm doing it, and I'm enjoying it more than I expected.

I'm grateful for how far I've come, and I'm grateful that I have the opportunity to keep learning.

*Poco a poco.*
