I recently reached 600 hours of Spanish input, which puts me at Level 5 on [the Dreaming Spanish roadmap][ds-roadmap].

I've been thinking about what has actually changed since I reached 300 hours. Obviously I understand more Spanish now. Most learner content is comfortable, some native content is accessible, and I've had crosstalk conversations lasting 50 minutes about politics, religion, philosophy, artificial intelligence, work, and whatever else came up while the other person spoke entirely in Spanish.

But the progress is still incredibly uneven.

I can follow the vast majority of a fast conversation between several familiar speakers and then watch two food critics discussing tapas in Madrid and understand around 40 percent. One person can feel almost effortless while another turns Spanish back into noise.

Reaching Level 5 has not made the language uniformly comprehensible.

The biggest change may be that I no longer fall apart as easily when comprehension becomes difficult.

If I had to turn the last 300 hours into advice, I would reduce it to five things.

## The advice

**Do not confuse discomfort with defeat.** Missing something does not mean the entire conversation is lost. A difficult stretch can remain difficult without becoming hopeless.

**Measure recovery, not only comprehension.** Understanding more is obviously important, but so is finding your way back after you lose the thread.

**Do not let one difficult speaker define your level.** Comprehension changes enormously with the speaker, subject, format, recording, and how focused or tired you are.

**Easy input is where comprehension becomes automatic.** Hard content reveals the edges of your ability, but comfortable content gives partially acquired language enough repetition to become fast and natural.

**Learn to repair instead of expecting a perfect performance.** Real communication depends on asking again, rephrasing, pointing, and admitting when something did not land.

These are not really separate lessons. They all involve learning to remain engaged when I do not completely understand what is happening.

This reminds me a lot of chess.

One thing I have always struggled with in chess is staying calm when a position starts looking shaky. Maybe my opponent plays a move I did not anticipate. Maybe I realize that one of my pieces is misplaced or that I am going to have to defend an unpleasant position for a while.

It is very easy in those moments to start behaving as though the game is already over. I stop looking for resources in the position. I assume every exchange favors my opponent. I play without much conviction because I have already decided that the situation is hopeless.

Sometimes the position really is lost. Often, though, it is merely uncomfortable.

The position is difficult, not lost.

If I cannot tolerate that discomfort, I can turn a position that was still playable into an actual defeat. The move I missed creates one problem. My reaction to missing it creates several more.

Spanish comprehension can unravel in almost exactly the same way.

## Discomfort is not defeat

Around 300 hours, missing a sentence could still feel catastrophic. I would become preoccupied with whatever I had missed and want to rewind, check subtitles, look something up, or mentally reconstruct the sentence in English.

Meanwhile, the speaker kept talking.

By the time I returned my attention to the audio, I had often missed several more sentences. The original gap may not have mattered very much, but my reaction to it caused the rest of the conversation to collapse.

Over time I have become much better at allowing incomplete understanding to remain incomplete. I can miss something, keep listening, and often reconnect a few seconds later. Sometimes the missing detail becomes clear from what follows. Sometimes I never figure it out and it turns out not to matter.

This is not the same as pretending I understood. It is simply accepting that I did not understand without letting that become the only thing I can think about.

I still have the impulse to force clarity. I notice it when I reach for subtitles during a difficult video or look up too many words while reading. Spoken language is especially uncomfortable because it disappears immediately. There is no sentence sitting on the screen or page patiently exposed for me to inspect it.

But native speech contains slang, jokes, cultural references, unfamiliar voices, unclear enunciation and sudden changes in subject. If I treat every gap as an emergency, listening becomes exhausting.

I do not have to feel confident or completely in control. I just have to remain mentally present while the position is uncomfortable.

## Finding my way back

I have spent a lot of time estimating comprehension percentages during this process. I will finish a video and decide that I understood 70 percent, 85 percent, or almost all of it.

Those estimates can be useful, but they miss one of the clearest improvements I've experienced between 300 and 600 hours.

My recovery time is much faster.

At an earlier stage, one missed phrase could knock me out of the next several sentences. Now I can often reconnect almost immediately. I may never recover the exact words I missed, but I recover the subject, the emotional tone, and the direction of the conversation.

After returning from Spain around 500 hours, I described this as being "in the middle of meaning." I could miss individual words while still knowing what was happening, what the speaker was doing, and where the conversation was broadly going.

That feels more practically important than understanding every word in perfect listening conditions.

It became especially obvious in Spain because real interactions do not come with a title, thumbnail, known subject, controlled audio, or rewind button. Someone says something while walking away. A waiter asks a question I was not expecting. A taxi driver moves from football to work to where I am from.

Sometimes I lost the beginning of an interaction and never completely recovered. But much more often than I expected, I found my way back into it.

Chess works similarly. If I get hit with a move I did not see, I do not always need to solve the entire position immediately. Maybe I just need to keep things together for another move, trade one dangerous piece, or force my opponent to make another decision.

In Spanish, sometimes I just need to catch the next sentence.

## Spanish is not one ability

Another lesson that has become increasingly clear is that my Spanish level is not one fixed number.

At 350 hours, I started deliberately listening to people who were historically difficult for me. Ester's Dominican accent was challenging. Michelle's cadence and softer delivery made it difficult for me to identify word boundaries. Andrés once sounded extremely dense and overwhelming.

By 600 hours, Ester's accent felt very comfortable. I could follow long conversations with her friends and family, including when they moved into a new subject, without feeling like I was drowning. Andrés had long since changed from overwhelming to fast but trackable.

At the same time, I recently watched one of the hardest stand up videos on Dreaming Spanish. I could get the gist, especially on a second viewing, but it was genuinely difficult. The problem was not just vocabulary. There was slang, sarcasm, exaggerated voices, shifting intonation and the general looseness of stand up comedy.

Then there was the native tapas video that knocked me down to around 40 percent.

Neither of those experiences erased the fact that I could follow most of a difficult group conversation with fast speech and laughter or discuss critical theory and politics during crosstalk.

A hard video tells me something about how my Spanish currently interacts with that speaker, subject, format, recording and my attention that day. It does not give me a final verdict on my Spanish.

This is true in chess too. I can feel comfortable in one type of position and miserable in another. An unfamiliar opening, closed position, tactical mess or time scramble can expose completely different weaknesses. One ugly game does not invalidate every good one, although it might show me something I need to work on.

I keep returning to the image of Spanish as a dark room filled with thousands of radios. Some are producing a clear signal. Others are still mostly static. Sometimes tuning one seems to help with several others.

An accent that sounds like static may simply need its own hours.

## Easy input still matters

The chess comparison applies here too.

Staying in the fight does not mean refusing to resign every position. Sometimes a chess position really is lost and there is no value in dragging it out. The important thing is being able to distinguish a genuinely lost position from one that is merely uncomfortable.

The same is true of input. I do not need to force myself to finish every difficult video. I no longer think difficulty is automatically valuable.

Some speakers are difficult but interesting enough that I want the challenge. Other content is hard in ways I do not find particularly compelling. If something is both incomprehensible and boring, I do not gain anything by treating it as a test of courage. I can turn it off and find something better.

Comfortable input has done an enormous amount of work for me.

After spending two weeks operating near the edge of my abilities in Spain, I returned to slower learner content. It did not feel like going backward. It felt like consolidation. Phrases that arrived two seconds too late during real interactions had room to become faster and more stable.

At 600 hours, a Dreaming Spanish podcast episode rated around 60 felt notably slow and easy. But it was still interesting, and I could follow the ideas without spending all my energy trying to decode the language. Partially acquired words and structures received another clear repetition. Things I already understood became more automatic.

Series have been especially useful for this. Once I settle into the speakers, subject, and format, I can stop worrying about the difficulty rating and just listen. Familiarity gives me time to adjust to someone's voice and allows vocabulary to repeat naturally.

Hard content reveals the edges of my ability. Comfortable content strengthens the enormous area inside those edges.

I need both. I do not need every listening session to become an ordeal.

## Successful but foggy

My trip to Spain between 450 and 500 hours gave me the clearest test of what all this input had actually produced.

The phrase I used afterward was "successful but foggy."

I could buy things, order food, ask questions, talk to taxi drivers, make small jokes, and follow parts of social conversations. My Spanish was real and practically useful. But from inside my own head, I often felt much less capable than I probably appeared. I was reconstructing, guessing, and hoping I had understood the important part.

Some of the most useful moments were the ones where the first attempt failed.

In a tobacco shop, I used the wrong word while trying to buy stamps and the woman pointed me toward postcards. I picked up a postcard, turned it over, pointed to the place where the stamp goes, and said I needed *this*. She said *sellos*, and the interaction continued.

During a taxi ride, I tried to ask whether the driver had a union job. He did not understand my first attempt, so I changed the question and asked whether he worked for himself or for a company. It was not especially elegant, but it worked well enough to reopen the conversation.

And then there was the restaurant where a waiter asked me something I did not understand and I responded with a panicked *sí*. A few minutes later, unwanted cheese arrived on my food.

That became one of the most practical lessons from the trip: never use *sí* as a panic button.

It is better to ask someone to repeat the question than to agree to a mystery option.

Real communication depends on asking again, narrowing the question, saying something a different way, pointing, using the physical context, or admitting that something did not land. Those are not substitutes for real Spanish. They are part of having a real conversation.

Crosstalk has given me another way to practice this without trying to force Spanish output before it comes naturally. My partner speaks Spanish and I generally respond in English. If I miss something, he repeats it or explains it differently in Spanish. We keep talking.

The goal is not to perform a lesson correctly. It is to understand another person well enough for the conversation to continue.

## Staying in the fight

Level 5 is not fluency, at least not according to any definition that would be meaningful to me.

There is still a huge amount of vocabulary I do not know. Spoken numbers continue to require a ridiculous amount of processing power. Native content remains inconsistent. My spontaneous output is far behind my comprehension. Another 400 hours separate me from the next Dreaming Spanish level.

But I have accumulated enough evidence that one difficult stretch no longer convinces me that the process has stopped working.

At 300 hours, staying in the fight often meant resisting the urge to rewind after a missed sentence. At 500 hours, it meant recovering from a misunderstanding in a shop or taxi. At 600 hours, it can mean following a complex conversation while accepting that some of the details will remain unresolved.

The goal is not to become comfortable with understanding nothing. It is to become more comfortable continuing when my understanding is incomplete.

That is the same lesson I keep encountering over the chessboard. The position may be unpleasant. I may not know what the next several moves should be. I may have overlooked something important and be forced to defend.

But discomfort is not defeat.

The position is difficult, not lost.

Stay in the fight.
