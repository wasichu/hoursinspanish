## Starting a collection

One of my favorite parts of acquiring Spanish has been collecting idioms, sayings, and clichés.

I mean the kinds of phrases that Spanish speakers use all the time but that often don't quite survive literal translation: *llorar a lágrima viva*[^lagrima-viva], *tener la mosca detrás de la oreja*[^mosca], *estar hecho polvo*[^polvo], *irse el santo al cielo*[^santo]. Some are funny, some are strange, and some are almost tiny poems in themselves.

For a while now, I've been adding to a running collection whenever I encounter one I like in books, videos, podcasts, or conversations. I've accumulated a couple dozen by now. I don't really study them as vocabulary. Mostly, I just enjoy noticing and collecting them.

Taken together, the collection has started to feel like a little private museum. Each phrase is interesting on its own, but putting them next to one another creates connections I didn't notice when I first encountered them.

Recently I decided to try doing something fun with them: write a song.

I've written before about [creating songs in Spanish]({{< relref "compartir/writing-songs-in-a-language-i-dont-know.md" >}}), but this was a more specific constraint. It was also a great way to dust off some of the sayings I've accumulated.

## A house waiting for someone

At the same time, I've been listening to [the latest album][bc-tmg-days] by The Mountain Goats. I'm a huge admirer of John Darnielle's writing, especially the way he can use a handful of concrete images to establish a setting, feeling, or implied story without spelling everything out.

I've also always been drawn to a particular image (not from The Mountain Goats): someone setting a table for a huge dinner or gathering, only for nobody to arrive.

That became the starting point for the verses.

Rather than tell a clear story, I wanted to create a house that seems to be waiting for people who never come back. The domestic objects gradually take on lives of their own:

> La casa duerme bajo el agua,
> la lámpara vela el lugar,
> mi sombra se cuelga de un cable,
> cada tarde vuelve a pesar.

And later:

> Las llaves roncan en el plato,
> las flores pierden el color,
> dimos el regreso por sentado,
> y el polvo aprendió el comedor.

I don't know exactly what happened in this house other than something vaguely awful and destructive, and I prefer it that way.

The images are supposed to suggest more than they explain.

## Then the idioms arrive

The chorus works very differently.

The verses are slower and leave space around the images. Then the chorus arrives and the idioms start coming almost rapid-fire:

> Lo hecho, hecho está,
> mi gozo, en un pozo,
> como quien oye llover,
> y nosotros mirando el destrozo.
>
> Se hizo lo que se pudo,
> salió por la culata,
> discutiendo por nada,
> el arroz se nos pasaba.

When looking through idioms to use, I realized that the ones I chose have a surprisingly consistent mood.

They're all a little fatalistic. They're all about trying to accept, or at least note, that everything's gone wrong.

That may be part of what makes idioms useful: they're ready-made language for moments when plain description feels inadequate. Here, each one lets the narrator acknowledge failure without ever having to explain it.

**Lo hecho, hecho está** — what's done is done.

**Mi gozo en un pozo** — something you were excited or hopeful about ends in disappointment.

**Como quien oye llover** — to hear something without really paying it any attention, as though you're simply listening to the rain.

**Se hizo lo que se pudo** — we did what we could.

**Salir por la culata** — for a plan to backfire or produce the opposite of what was intended.

Together, they start to sound like a series of small acts of resignation. Nobody explains exactly what went wrong. Instead, the chorus just keeps producing new ways of acknowledging that something did.

That ended up fitting the house in the verses remarkably well.

## Bending the idioms a little

A couple of the expressions also made their way into the song less directly.

In:

> dimos el regreso por sentado

the phrase *dar por sentado* means to take something for granted or assume it to be certain.

What gets taken for granted here is *el regreso* itself: the idea that someone or something would come back.

And then there's the last line of the chorus:

> el arroz se nos pasaba

which plays with *pasársele el arroz a alguien*.

The idiom is about letting the right moment or opportunity pass you by. But inside this particular song, the line can also work literally. There's a house, a table, a meal, people who aren't arriving — and while everyone keeps arguing, the rice is overcooking.

I especially like those moments because the idiom gains another resonance and feels like it really belongs to the little world of the song itself.

## From collecting to using

That was probably the part of the experiment I found most satisfying.

I wasn't trying to write a "song for learning Spanish idioms," and I didn't want to cram twenty expressions into the lyrics just because I'd collected them.

I wanted to see what happened when some of these phrases I'd been encountering for months collided with other things I like: music, images, ambiguity, and incomplete little stories.

At first, an idiom is something I hear in Spanish and think: *that's a great phrase*. I save it because I figure it'll be useful someday.

Then, eventually, one of those phrases ends up inside something I've made.
